If a technician just told you that your furnace has a "cracked heat exchanger," you've probably got two questions: what does that actually mean, and how worried should you be? Fair questions. The heat exchanger is one of those furnace parts most homeowners never think about until it fails — and when it does, it's not a part you want to ignore.

## What a Heat Exchanger Actually Does

Inside your furnace, the heat exchanger is a series of metal chambers or tubes that separate the combustion process from the air that circulates through your home. Natural gas burns inside the exchanger, heating the metal walls. Your blower motor then pushes house air across those hot surfaces, warming it before sending it through your ductwork.

The key word there is "separate." The combustion byproducts — including carbon monoxide — stay on one side of that metal wall, while the air you breathe stays on the other. As long as the exchanger is intact, that separation holds. That's the whole job of the part.

## Why Cracks Happen

Heat exchangers go through constant thermal cycling — heating up every time the furnace runs, cooling down every time it shuts off. Over years of this expansion and contraction, especially in a furnace that's 15-20+ years old, metal fatigue sets in. Rust from condensation, oversized or undersized equipment that cycles too often, and restricted airflow (often from a dirty filter or closed vents) can all accelerate the process.

A small crack might start as nothing more than a hairline fracture. But once it's there, it tends to grow — and it only takes a small gap for combustion gases to leak into your supply air during the heat exchanger's expansion cycle.

## Signs of a Heat Exchanger Problem

Cracked exchangers aren't always obvious, which is part of what makes them concerning. Watch for:

- A carbon monoxide detector alarm (take this seriously, always)
- A strange odor similar to formaldehyde when the furnace runs
- Soot buildup around the furnace cabinet
- The burner flame flickering or changing color/shape when the blower kicks on
- Headaches, nausea, or flu-like symptoms that improve when you leave the house

If you notice any of these, especially a working CO detector going off, get everyone out of the house and into fresh air, then call for help. Don't wait to investigate it yourself.

## How Technicians Find the Problem

A visual inspection alone often isn't enough — cracks can be hidden deep inside the exchanger's chambers. Techs typically use a combination of methods: a borescope camera to look inside the chambers directly, combustion analyzers to check for abnormal CO readings in the flue, and sometimes a smoke test or pressure test that can reveal leaks a visual check would miss. This is exactly why an annual [HVAC maintenance](/hvac-maintenance/) visit matters — a trained eye catches early warning signs before they become safety issues.

## What Happens Next

If a heat exchanger is confirmed cracked, there's no safe repair for it. The part itself can sometimes be replaced if the furnace is young and the exchanger is still under warranty, but on an older unit, replacing just that component often costs nearly as much as a new furnace — and the rest of the system is likely nearing the end of its life anyway. Most of the time, a cracked exchanger means it's time to talk about [furnace installation](/furnace-installation/) rather than furnace repair.

## Protecting Your Home in the Meantime

Whether or not you've had a heat exchanger issue, a few habits lower your risk significantly:

- Install CO detectors on every level of your home, especially near sleeping areas
- Keep up with annual furnace maintenance — this is when exchanger issues get caught early
- Change your furnace filter regularly to keep airflow (and thermal stress) in check
- Never ignore unusual smells, sounds, or a flickering burner flame
