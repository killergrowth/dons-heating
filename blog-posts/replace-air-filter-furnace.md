If you've ever stood in front of your furnace holding a new filter and wondered which way it's supposed to go, you're not alone. It's one of the simplest maintenance jobs in your entire home, and it's also one of the most commonly skipped — until airflow drops, energy bills climb, or your furnace starts working harder than it should.

Here in Central Kansas, where our furnaces run hard through long winter stretches, a clean filter isn't just a nice-to-have. It directly affects how efficiently your system heats your home and how long it lasts. Let's walk through exactly how to do it right.

## Why Your Furnace Filter Matters More Than You Think

Your furnace filter does one job: it traps dust, pet dander, pollen, and debris before that air circulates through your ductwork and back into your living spaces. A clogged filter restricts airflow, which forces your blower motor to work overtime. That extra strain can shorten the life of your system and spike your utility bills.

A dirty filter is also one of the most common reasons we get called out for [furnace repair](/furnace-repair/) calls that turn out to be preventable. Overheating, short-cycling, and even frozen evaporator coils in the cooling season can often be traced back to restricted airflow from a filter that should've been swapped out weeks earlier.

## Step 1: Find Your Filter and Check the Size

Most residential furnaces have the filter located in one of two spots: a slot built into the return air duct near the furnace, or inside the blower compartment itself. If you're not sure where yours is, check your owner's manual or look for a thin metal or cardboard-framed slot near where the ductwork connects to the unit.

Once you find it, slide the old filter out and check the printed size on the cardboard frame (something like 16x25x1 or 20x20x1). Buy a matching size — an ill-fitting filter won't seal properly, letting unfiltered air bypass it entirely.

## Step 2: Check the MERV Rating

MERV (Minimum Efficiency Reporting Value) ratings range from 1 to 20 and tell you how effectively a filter captures particles. For most homes, a MERV 8 to MERV 11 filter strikes a good balance between filtration and airflow. Going too high (MERV 13+) on a system that wasn't designed for it can actually restrict airflow and strain your blower motor — so if you're considering an upgrade for allergies or air quality concerns, it's worth asking a technician first. If indoor air quality is a bigger priority for your household, you might also look into [indoor air quality](/indoor-air-quality/) solutions designed to work alongside your existing system rather than replacing airflow capacity with a filter alone.

## Step 3: Note the Airflow Direction

This is the step most homeowners get wrong. Every filter has an arrow printed on its frame indicating airflow direction. That arrow should point *toward* the furnace (in the direction air flows through the system), not away from it. Installing it backward reduces filtration efficiency and can cause the filter to collapse under airflow pressure over time.

Take a photo of the arrow direction before you pull the old filter so you have a quick reference when the new one goes in.

## Step 4: Slide In the New Filter

With the furnace powered down (flip the switch near the unit or at your breaker panel if you're not confident it'll shut off automatically), slide the new filter in along the same track the old one came out of. It should fit snugly without forcing — if it's tight enough to bend or crimp, double-check your sizing.

## Step 5: Set a Reminder for Next Time

Standard 1-inch filters typically need replacing every 1 to 3 months, depending on your household — more often if you have pets, allergies, or run your system constantly during peak season. Thicker media filters (4–5 inch) can often go 6 to 12 months between changes. Setting a recurring phone reminder tied to the season change is the easiest way to stay consistent.

## When It's More Than Just the Filter

If you've been diligent about filter changes and you're still noticing weak airflow, uneven heating, or rising energy bills, the issue may be elsewhere in the system. Dirty ductwork, an aging blower motor, or a furnace nearing the end of its service life can all mimic the symptoms of a clogged filter. That's a good time to schedule a [HVAC maintenance](/hvac-maintenance/) visit so a technician can look at the whole picture, not just the filter slot.
