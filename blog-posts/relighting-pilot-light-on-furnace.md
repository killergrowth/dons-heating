If you've walked over to your thermostat expecting heat and gotten nothing but cold air, a pilot light that's gone out might be the culprit. It's one of the most common furnace complaints we see in El Dorado and across Central Kansas, especially when the first real cold snap of the season hits and a furnace that's been sitting idle all summer finally gets put to work.

The good news: sometimes relighting a pilot light is a simple, safe fix. The not-so-good news: sometimes a pilot light that won't stay lit is a symptom of something more serious, and trying to force it back on isn't the right move. Here's how to tell the difference.

## Why Your Pilot Light Went Out in the First Place

A pilot light doesn't just blow out for no reason. Common causes include:

- **A draft** — a gust from a door, window, or even the furnace's own air intake can snuff it out
- **A dirty or misaligned pilot orifice** — dust and debris build up over months of inactivity
- **A failing thermocouple** — this small safety sensor detects the pilot flame and shuts off gas flow if it doesn't sense heat; when it wears out, it can cause the pilot to go out repeatedly
- **A cracked or dirty flame sensor** — similar symptom, different part depending on your furnace's age and type
- **A gas supply issue** — low gas pressure or a partially closed valve

If this is the first time it's happened, it's often harmless. If it's happening every few days, that's your furnace telling you something needs attention.

## How to Relight a Standing Pilot Light

Many older furnaces (and some newer ones) use a standing pilot light system. If yours does, you'll typically find a small door or panel near the bottom of the furnace labeled with lighting instructions. Here's the general process:

1. **Turn the gas control knob to "Off"** and wait at least 5 minutes for any lingering gas to clear
2. **Locate the pilot light assembly**, usually visible through the access panel
3. **Turn the knob to "Pilot"** and press it down
4. **While holding the knob down, light the pilot** using a long lighter or match, or press the igniter button if your unit has one
5. **Hold the knob down for about 30-60 seconds** after the flame catches, to let the thermocouple heat up
6. **Release the knob slowly** — if the flame stays lit, turn the knob to "On"

If the flame goes out as soon as you release the knob, that's almost always a thermocouple issue, not something you did wrong.

## When Relighting Isn't the Right Call

A few situations where we'd tell you to stop and call us instead of attempting a DIY fix:

- **You smell gas** before, during, or after attempting to relight the pilot — leave the house and call your gas utility immediately, then call us
- **The pilot won't stay lit** even after a successful relighting attempt and a full thermocouple heat-up cycle
- **The flame looks wrong** — a healthy pilot flame should be steady and mostly blue with maybe a small yellow tip; a flickering, mostly yellow, or orange flame can indicate a combustion problem
- **Your furnace is older than 15-20 years** and this is becoming a recurring issue — at that point, you're often better served looking at [signs you need a new furnace](/blog/signs-you-need-a-new-furnace/) rather than continuing to patch an aging system
- **You're just not comfortable working around a gas appliance** — there's no shame in that, and we'd rather you call than feel pressured to do it yourself

Modern high-efficiency furnaces largely use electronic ignition instead of a standing pilot, so if your furnace is newer, "pilot light" issues usually point to a different ignition or control board problem entirely — one more reason a quick diagnostic from a technician beats guessing.

## What a Recurring Pilot Problem Usually Costs to Fix

A thermocouple replacement is typically one of the more affordable repairs we handle — it's a small part and a straightforward swap for a trained tech. If the issue turns out to be something bigger, like a cracked heat exchanger or failing gas valve, costs go up, but so does the importance of catching it early.
